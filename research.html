The Zero Pattern of a Design Matrix Drives Multiple Descent in Over-parameterized Regression

Kevin Han Huang, Haoyu Ye, Somak Laha, Morgane Austern

Over-parameterized models often predict well even when they interpolate the training data, and plotted against model size the test error can fall, peak at the interpolation threshold, then fall again. Work producing more than one peak has usually put them there by hand, through the architecture or separate feature blocks or distinct kernel scales. We wanted to know when several peaks come out of the data alone.

We study ridge least squares with a vanishing penalty, which approximates the minimum-norm interpolator and is also what numerical implementations actually compute, in the proportional regime. The designs we consider can accommodate degeneracies and dependencies. Data augmentation is an instance of the second kind. We reduce the designs to their variance profile and study the profile directly. A zero entry marks a direction that one observation cannot see, so the degeneracies of the problem are recorded as a pattern of zeros, and that pattern is what the title points at.

A single design with a single estimator can show several risk peaks with nothing tuned to place them. The driver is spectral degeneracy rather than heterogeneity on its own, since positive-definite covariances give only the classical peak at ratio one however heterogeneous they are. And one combinatorial rule related to the bipartite graph of the support of the variance profile fixes where the peaks of the risk curve sit. In short, the bias sits on coordinates that some maximum matching leaves unmatched, and a variance peak occurs where the residual matching becomes rigid, both readable off the Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition. Proving it needed a new random-matrix ingredient, an extension of a local law that tracks the spectral parameter explicitly so that it survives a ridge shrinking to zero.

Graph Attention Network for Node Regression on Random Geometric Graphs with Erdős–Rényi Contamination Submitted, NeurIPS 2026

Somak Laha, Suqi Liu, Morgane Austern

Graph attention networks let a node weight its neighbours instead of averaging over all of them, and they are now a default choice for node-level prediction. Whether attention provably helps is much less settled. Most of the theory concerns expressivity, meaning which neighbourhood functions an attention layer can represent, and says little about statistical risk.

We picked a setting where the question has a clean answer. Every node carries a latent covariate and a response generated linearly from it, but the covariate is only observed with noise. The graph is a random dot-product graph, but contaminated with independent Erdős–Rényi edges in observation. The geometric edges are informative about the true neighbours, while the Erdős–Rényi ones are not.

Regressing the response on the noisy covariate is an errors-in-variables problem, so least squares carries attenuation bias and is inconsistent. Averaging over every neighbour is spoiled by the spurious edges instead. Our layer screens neighbours by thresholding dot products, keeps the ones that look geometric, and averages their covariates into a proxy for the latent one.

Designing that screening step took some care. Using the same coordinates to select neighbours and to average them makes the selection correlated with the measurement noise, and hence with the regression residuals, which biases the estimate.

Regressing on the proxies is consistent, and the asymptotic prediction error at an unlabelled node approaches the observation-noise variance even under heavy contamination. Any finite-depth network aggregating raw neighbourhoods does strictly worse once the Erdős–Rényi degree dominates the geometric one. The proofs lean on high-dimensional geometric tail bounds and concentration for neighbourhood counts and sample covariances, and the synthetic sweeps behave as the theory says they should.

Symmetry breaking and collective bias in populations of LLM agents

With Prof. Subhabrata Sen, Harvard University

Ask one language model which letter of the alphabet it prefers and the answer is close to uniform. Put a population of them in a naming game, where agents are paired at random, and they settle on a shared convention without any coordination. The convention itself is not uniform over the options. Some letters win far more often than others.

So a bias too small to see in any single agent is presumably being amplified by repeated communication, and we would like to know how small it can be and still win. We model the population with Quantized Simplex Gossip, where each agent holds an internal distribution over the options and updates it from the finite samples it sees from its partners. We start the agents a very small distance off symmetry and follow what happens to that initial asymmetry.

Depreferential attachment through preferential attachment

With Prof. Antar Bandyopadhyay, Indian Statistical Institute · M.Stat. dissertation and continuing work

In the Barabási–Albert model an arriving vertex attaches to existing ones in proportion to their degree, so the largest vertices grow fastest and the degrees follow a power law. Plenty of real systems work against that. A marketplace rests its best sellers instead of promoting them, and the most prominent people in a field are the hardest ones to reach, so newcomers end up a tier down.

This model damps the leaders: at each step some old vertices are sampled in a degree-biased way and declared taboo, and the arriving vertex then does an ordinary preferential attachment step among the rest. A varying taboo budget sets how hard the leaders are held back.

For the dissertation I bounded the maximal degree under hard-core tabooing, and bounded the growth of a fixed vertex's degree and of the degree distribution under the soft-core scheme.

Since then we have sharpened all of this, and added separate offsets for the two stages. A fixed vertex now grows polynomially, logarithmically or doubly logarithmically depending on how fast the budget grows, with a martingale CLT in each case. The bulk degree distribution results for soft-core tabooing have been sharpened as well, for each of the relevant regimes.

Edge-preserving regression surface fitting

With Prof. Partha Sarathi Mukherjee, Indian Statistical Institute · with Riddhiman Saha and Souhardya Ray

Kernel smoothing fits a surface by averaging over a neighbourhood of each point. Where the true surface has a jump it averages straight across and the discontinuity disappears, which is often the thing you actually wanted: a boundary in an image, a fault in elevation data, a front in a temperature field.

Following Qiu and Yandell, we look for a jump before smoothing rather than after. In each small neighbourhood we test whether one plane or two fits the data better. Two planes winning means an edge runs through the neighbourhood, and we then estimate along which line, so the smoothing can be restricted to the points on the same side of it as the centre. Where no edge is found, the ordinary smoother is used unchanged.

On simulated surfaces with jumps this beats plain local polynomial smoothing on mean squared error, and it keeps the edges visibly sharper.
